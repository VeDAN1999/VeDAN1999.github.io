# Daniel Baldwin

<p class="lede">Applied mathematician. I work on stochastic processes and path integrals, in particular the complex saddles and Lefschetz thimbles that quietly govern real-valued rare events, and now on quantitative problems for clients at a boutique consultancy.</p>

I hold a PhD in Applied Mathematics from the University of Leeds (Leeds Doctoral Scholarship), where I developed a Picard–Lefschetz framework for stochastic path integrals in the weak-noise limit, followed by a research fellowship. Before that: BSc and MMath in Mathematics at Leeds, where I was awarded the Top 10 Scholarship and the HL Price Prize for the best Master's thesis. I'm now a quant consultant at [d-fine](https://www.d-fine.com) in London, a boutique management consultancy that specialises in quantitative problems.

This site collects my [research](/research/), a few [projects](/projects/) with code, and [write-ups](/writing/) of puzzles and problems I find interesting. My interests run from complex analysis, stochastic processes, asymptotics and probability to PDEs, ODEs and applied mathematics in general: anything where a good calculation changes the picture.

<p class="eyebrow" style="margin-top:2.5rem">My PhD in one breath</p>

<blockquote class="epigraph">Take a perfectly real stochastic process, a particle diffusing in a well: real time, real noise, real force, real probabilities. Write it as a path integral. Beneath it sits a complex skeleton: the path that matters most lives in the complex plane! Itô's formulation produces such paths naturally, and with them the analytic continuation of the stochastic path integral, <span class="cb-math">D → −D</span>, finally makes sense. A quasi-zero-mode integral that was never conceptually understood is resolved simply by following the paths off the real axis. Finding something this beautiful made the PhD worth it.</blockquote>
