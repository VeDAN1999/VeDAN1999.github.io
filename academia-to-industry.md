# From academia to the real world

<p class="lede">I came to university planning to do a good BSc and go into tech or finance. I stayed for over eight years, left as a research fellow, and ended up somewhere I had never heard of.</p>

## How I got here

The plan was simple: smash the BSc, leave. Instead I stayed all the way through to a research fellowship, almost entirely because of a brilliant supervisor. If you had told first-year me that I would end up at a boutique management consultancy, flying around Europe to meet clients, I would have asked what management consulting was and why on earth a maths PhD would end up there.

During the PhD I spent a lot of time thinking about what came next: quant research, staying in academia, defence technology, national labs, AI and ML roles. Then, by a stroke of fortune, I walked past the d-fine stand at a STEM PhD careers fair. I had no idea how competitive those roles are, and in hindsight not knowing was probably for the best. What hooked me was the people, nearly all maths and physics PhDs, and the sheer range of the work: pricing options on a trading floor one month, building software for a central bank the next, stochastic modelling of fungal growth for a botanical garden the one after. Three interviews, back to back, in three hours, in person. An offer followed, I finished the fellowship, and relocated to the edge of London.

A side story from the fellowship, because it was the highlight: a day spent in Carl Bender's flat at Imperial with Steve, during which Carl became so impressed with Steve that he told him, in language I will not print here, exactly how clever he thought he was.

## The first few months

Things move quickly here. Really quickly. I joined, spent two weeks in Frankfurt in a brutal heat wave, and was then flown across Europe to my first client, a central bank. Within a month I had finished my first project, moving data out of a legacy system that was being decommissioned into its replacement. I am now reworking that system's permissioning and authentication for fine-grained access control, which means a great deal of C# written by someone who had never touched C# before.

The team has been lovely, the learning curve is as steep as anything in the PhD, and the variety is exactly what I wanted. Two things I miss: the fifteen-minute walk to the department, now an hour and a quarter by train, and the rhythm of research, where a hard problem is allowed to take as long as it takes.

## What carried over

- The instinct to find the one calculation that changes the picture, and to do it properly.
- Being comfortable with being confused for a long time before anything becomes clear.
- Writing things down so that someone else can check them.

## What didn't

- The timescale. A PhD problem can take a year; a client question needs an answer by Thursday, and a good answer on Thursday beats a complete one in March.
- The audience. Nobody on a project cares that the saddle is complex. They care what the number means and how much to trust it.

## What I had to learn fast

- Cloud infrastructure, databases, authentication, and the tooling around all of it, none of which appears in a maths PhD.
- How a decision actually gets made inside a large organisation, and where a quantitative argument fits into that.
