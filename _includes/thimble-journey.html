1 · A thimble is a curve through a point

Start with one complex variable. To estimate $\int e^{-S(z)/4D}\,dz$ for small $D$, deform the contour so that it passes through a saddle $z_\sigma$ of $S$ along the path of steepest descent: the curve on which $\operatorname{Im} S$ is constant and $\operatorname{Re} S$ grows as fast as possible away from the saddle. That curve is the Lefschetz thimble $\mathcal{J}_\sigma$. Its mirror image — the curve of steepest ascent — is the dual thimble $\mathcal{K}_\sigma$. The original contour equals $\sum_\sigma n_\sigma \mathcal{J}_\sigma$, and $n_\sigma$ simply counts how many times it crosses $\mathcal{K}_\sigma$.

2 · In function space, a thimble is a manifold through a path

Now the integral is over paths $x(t)$ and the saddle $z_\sigma$ is itself a path — the stochastic complex bounce. The thimble is no longer a curve but a manifold: the set of all paths that flow down onto the bounce under the gradient flow of $h = \operatorname{Re}\mathcal{S}/4D$, with $\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{S}$ constant along every flow line. Near the bounce it splits into three kinds of direction: an exact zero mode (time translation $t_c$, perfectly flat), the quasi-zero mode $\theta$ (the instanton–anti-instanton separation, almost flat), and the stable Gaussian modes (steep).

3 · Cut it along the quasi-zero mode

The Gaussian directions are harmless: integrate them on their real cycles and they give a determinant. The exact zero mode gives the time $t$. Everything subtle lives in a single direction, so cut the manifold along the $\theta$ line, keep that one-dimensional sheet, and flatten it out. What remains is an ordinary contour integral in the complex $\theta$-plane,

$$I_{\mathrm{QZM}} = \int_{\mathcal{J}_\sigma} d\theta\, \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\mathcal{V}_-(\theta)}{4D}\Big), \qquad \frac{\mathcal{V}_-(\theta)}{4D} = \omega\theta - \frac{8a^3}{D}\,e^{-\omega\theta}.$$

4 · Push the contour off the real line

On the real $\theta$-axis the integrand is $\exp(+\tfrac{8a^3}{D}e^{-\omega\theta})$: the instanton and anti-instanton attract, and the integral diverges as $\theta \to -\infty$ (the bright region). The critical points sit at $\theta^* = \tfrac{1}{\omega}\log\tfrac{8a^3}{D} \pm i\pi/\omega$, and the steepest-descent contours through them are the horizontal lines $\mathcal{J}_\sigma = \mathbb{R} \pm i\pi/\omega$. A small regulator $\nu$ decides which dual thimble the real axis crosses, so $(n_0, n_1) = (0,1)$ or $(1,0)$: exactly one of the two shifted lines contributes, and the sign $e^{\pm i\pi}$ is the geometric avatar of the BZJ ambiguity.

5 · The integral is now exact

With $\xi = e^{-\omega\theta}$ the shifted contour maps onto the positive real $\xi$-axis and the integrand becomes a pure exponential:

$$I_{\mathrm{QZM}} = \frac{e^{\pm i\pi}}{\omega}\int_0^\infty d\xi\, e^{-(8a^3/D)\,\xi} = \frac{e^{\pm i\pi}}{\omega}\Big(\frac{D}{8a^3}\Big)\,\Gamma(1).$$

The naive Gaussian around the same critical point gives the same answer multiplied by $\sqrt{2\pi}/e$ — the "peculiar error factor" of the older literature is nothing but Stirling's approximation to $\Gamma(1)$, sneaking in from a contour that was never the right one. The thimble removes it exactly, and Kramers' prefactor comes out correct.
